### Introduction

A hiring manager looking at your application has a few minutes and one question: can this person do the job? A resume claims you can. Public work shows it. Learning in public means sharing what you build and learn as you go, through blog posts, READMEs, short write-ups and demos. For career changers without a prior AI job, it's the most reliable way to build credibility, and it compounds over time.

This lesson covers why it works and how to start without feeling like a fraud.

### Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- What learning in public means, and why it works for getting hired.
- Getting past "I'm not an expert yet."
- What to write about: TILs, project write-ups and experiment results.
- Writing for a technical reader: specific, short and evidence-first.
- Where to publish, and owning your own site.
- Building a sustainable habit.

### Key ideas

- **Public work is evidence.** A post that explains how you cut an agent's cost by 60% with measured before-and-after numbers says more than a resume bullet ever can. It also gives interviewers something concrete to ask about.
- **You don't need to be an expert, just one step ahead.** Write for the person who is where you were last month. Honest notes about what confused you and how you got unstuck are useful precisely because experts have forgotten what was hard.
- **Small, frequent posts beat rare, perfect ones.** A "today I learned" (TIL) note can be three paragraphs. A project write-up explains a problem, what you tried, and the measured result. Experiment results ("I compared three chunking strategies; here are the numbers") are especially valuable in AI, where everything changes fast.
- **Lead with evidence and specifics.** Show the code, the numbers, the eval scores and the failures. "It works well" is not a finding; "accuracy went from 71% to 86% on 200 held-out examples" is.
- **Own your home base.** Post wherever you like, but keep the canonical copy on your own site, which you'll build in Project: Portfolio Site, so your work doesn't disappear when a platform changes.
- **Make it a habit, not a campaign.** One short post every week or two, tied to whatever you're building, is sustainable. Sharing good work by others, with your own commentary, counts too.

### Assignment

<div class="lesson-content__panel" markdown="1">

1. Read [Learn in Public by swyx](https://www.swyx.io/learn-in-public), the essay that popularized the idea among developers.
   - Note the specific forms of public learning it suggests, and pick the two that suit you best.
1. Read [What to blog about by Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2022/Nov/6/what-to-blog-about/), which argues for two easy post types: things you learned and descriptions of projects.
1. Browse [Simon Willison's blog](https://simonwillison.net/) as a model of learning in public done consistently for years.
   - Read three of his short "TIL"-style or project posts and notice how short and specific they are.
1. Write and publish your first post: a 300 to 600 word TIL or project write-up about something you built in this curriculum. Include at least one code snippet or measured number. Publish it anywhere public for now; you'll move it onto your own site in Project: Portfolio Site.
