### Introduction

You have a portfolio with case studies, evidence behind every project and, if Open Source went well, a merged pull request. That's a strong position. But a strong position doesn't help much if nobody looks at it. Large companies can get hundreds of applications for a junior role within days, and a stranger's application has to get past a quick skim before anyone clicks a link. An application that arrives with a word from someone the team already trusts gets read.

That is what this section is about. Many junior hires come through people: a referral from an employee, a conversation at a meetup, a maintainer who remembers your pull request, a former colleague who moved into tech. None of that is luck, and none of it requires you to be outgoing or well connected today. It's a skill, and like the rest of this curriculum it gets easier with practice.

This lesson covers where AI engineers gather, how to ask for advice in a way people are glad to answer, how to write a short cold message, how to follow up without being pushy, and how referrals actually work. In Project: Outreach Sprint you'll put all of it into practice and measure what happens.

An honest note before we start: the market for entry-level and career-changer roles is hard right now, and no networking technique changes that on its own. What networking does is turn your evidence into conversations, and conversations into chances. It improves your odds; it doesn't guarantee an outcome.

### Why people matter in hiring

Look at hiring from the other side of the table. A hiring manager with one open role has to pick, from a pile of strangers, the few worth an hour of an engineer's time. Every interview costs the team time, and a bad hire costs far more. So hiring managers look for anything that reduces risk, and the strongest signal they can get is "someone I trust has worked with or talked to this person and thinks they're worth meeting."

A **referral** is exactly that signal, made formal. An employee submits your name through the company's internal system, and your application is flagged as referred. At many companies referred candidates get a guaranteed human read, and some companies pay employees a bonus when a referral is hired. A referral doesn't get you the job. You still go through the same interviews. But it often gets you past the first filter, which is where most applications stop.

You might worry that this is unfair, or that it means only people with connections get hired. It's true that people who already know engineers have an advantage. The way to close that gap is to meet engineers, which is open to anyone willing to show up, be useful, and ask well. Career changers often have an advantage here they don't notice: you know people in your old field, some of whom work at companies that now hire AI engineers, and you understand problems that pure engineers don't.

### Networking without the awkwardness

The word "networking" makes a lot of people picture handing out business cards and pretending to be interested. Drop that picture. For engineers, networking mostly means three ordinary things:

1. **Being where the work is discussed**, such as a forum, a Discord server, an issue tracker or a meetup, and paying attention.
1. **Being useful in public**: answering a question, sharing a result, fixing a small thing.
1. **Talking to people one at a time** about work you're both interested in.

None of this needs you to be extroverted. Written channels such as issue threads, forums and direct messages suit quieter people well, and a thoughtful written reply is often remembered longer than small talk.

Think of it as a long game. The point isn't to extract a job from each person you meet. It's to become someone a few people in the field know, recognize and would vouch for. Relationships built that way keep working for your whole career, long after this job search ends.

### Where AI engineers gather

You need a short list of places to show up regularly, not a long list of places to visit once.

**Open source projects.** You already did this in Open Source. The issue tracker and discussion forum of a library you use are full of engineers who build AI tools for a living. Maintainers notice contributors who are helpful and careful. Staying active in one project, answering issues, reviewing small PRs or improving docs, builds a reputation that a single PR can't.

**Online communities.** Many AI tools run a Discord server or forum, and those are good places to learn and help. Examples include the [Hugging Face forums](https://discuss.huggingface.co/), the [MLOps Community](https://mlops.community/) with its Slack and local chapters, and the Discords of the frameworks and eval tools you've used. Pick two, not ten. Read for a week before posting, follow each community's rules, and never paste the same message into many channels.

**Local meetups.** Search [Meetup](https://www.meetup.com/) and local tech calendars for AI, Python, data or "LLM" groups in your city. Many cities have monthly AI engineering or Python meetups that are free and friendly to newcomers. Going three times to the same meetup is worth more than going once to three different ones, because people start to recognize you. Most meetups also want speakers, and a ten-minute lightning talk about one of your projects is one of the fastest ways to be remembered.

**Conferences, without paying full price.** Tickets for big AI conferences can cost hundreds or thousands of dollars, but there are free ways in:

- **Volunteer.** Many conferences, especially community-run ones such as the [PyCon](https://pycon.org/) events held around the world, need volunteers for registration or session rooms, and many give them free or discounted admission. Policies vary, so check each event's volunteer page. It's also an easy way to meet organizers and speakers.
- **Financial aid and diversity scholarships.** Community conferences often offer grants for tickets and travel. Applications usually open months before the event.
- **Free tracks and expo passes.** Some events offer free expo-hall passes, free community days or free online streams.
- **Recorded talks.** Many conferences publish talks for free afterward. The [AI Engineer](https://www.ai.engineer/) conferences post theirs on YouTube. Watching talks won't meet anyone, but a specific comment on a speaker's talk is a great reason to write to them.

**Your own posts.** Since Learning in Public, you've been publishing a post per path. Posts bring people to you. When someone replies to a post or shares it, that's a warm connection: they already know your work.

**People you already know.** Former colleagues, classmates, friends of friends. Make a list of everyone you know who works in software, or at a company that uses AI. This is usually the most productive list you have, and the most overlooked.

### Give before you ask

The most reliable way to make people glad to hear from you is to have been useful to them, or to people like them, first. Giving doesn't need to be grand:

- Answer a newcomer's question in a community you belong to. You're one step ahead of someone, as you learned in Learning in Public.
- Reproduce a bug someone reported and add the details to the issue.
- Write up your notes from a meetup talk and send the speaker the link.
- Try a technique from someone's blog post on your own eval set and tell them the result, good or bad.
- Thank an author for a specific thing that helped you, with no request attached.

That last one matters more than it seems. People who write tutorials, maintain libraries or give talks rarely hear what their work did for someone. A message that says "your post on judge validation is why my judge agrees with my labels 90% of the time; here's my write-up" is a gift. It also happens to start a relationship, which is fine, as long as the thanks would be worth sending even if nothing came of it.

### Writing a short cold message

A **cold message** is a message to someone who doesn't know you. Most go unanswered. That's normal, and it's mostly not about you: people are busy, and inboxes are full. You can't control whether someone replies, but you can make replying easy and pleasant.

A good cold message has four parts and fits on a phone screen, under about 120 words:

1. **Who you are, in one line.** Not your life story. "I'm a former support lead learning AI engineering, and I build agents with eval suites."
1. **Why this person, specifically.** Proof that you're writing to them, not to a list: something they wrote, built, said in a talk or answered in a forum.
1. **One small, clear ask.** Usually a question they can answer in a few minutes, or a 20-minute call. Never "Can you get me a job?" and never several asks at once.
1. **An easy out.** "No worries if you're too busy" tells them a non-reply is fine, which oddly makes a reply more likely.

Here's a weak message:

```text
Hi! I'm passionate about AI and looking for opportunities. I'd love to
connect and pick your brain sometime. Are you hiring? I've attached my
resume. Thanks!
```

It could have been sent to anyone. "Pick your brain" asks for an open-ended chunk of time with no clear topic, "Are you hiring?" asks a stranger to do work for you, and the attachment adds pressure. Here's a stronger one:

```text
Hi Priya, I watched your talk on evaluating support agents, and your point
about grading environment state instead of transcripts changed how I test
mine. I'm a former support lead moving into agent engineering; my agent now
passes 27 of 30 eval trials, write-up here: yourname.github.io/ticket-agent

One question, if you have a minute: when your team started running agent
evals in CI, how did you decide which tasks were worth the cost of running
on every pull request?

No worries at all if you're too busy to reply. Thanks for the talk either way.
```

It's specific, it proves the sender paid attention, it shows evidence without bragging, and the question is one the recipient is uniquely placed to answer. Notice also what's missing: the message doesn't open with a bare "Hi!" and wait for a reply. Put the whole message in one go; the short page [no hello](https://nohello.net/) explains why that matters in chat.

Some practical rules:

- **Use the channel the person uses.** If they're active on a forum or in a project's discussions, a public reply there may be better than a private message. Check whether they say how they prefer to be contacted.
- **Write it yourself.** It's fine to ask an AI model to point out vague phrases or to shorten a draft. A message the model wrote from scratch reads like it, and the specific detail that makes a cold message work can only come from you.
- **Ask questions you couldn't answer with a search.** Julia Evans's [How to ask good questions](https://jvns.ca/blog/good-questions/) is the best short guide to this.

### Informational conversations

An **informational conversation** (often called an informational interview) is a short call where you ask someone about their work, their team or their path. It isn't a job interview, and you shouldn't treat it as a disguised one. Done well, it's one of the best ways to learn what a role is really like, and people often enjoy it, because you're asking them to talk about what they know.

Make it easy and worth their time:

- **Keep it short.** Ask for 20 minutes, and end on time even if it's going well. They can offer to keep talking.
- **Fit their schedule.** Offer two or three time slots in their time zone, or send a scheduling link.
- **Prepare three or four questions.** Good ones are specific to them: "What does a normal week look like on your team?", "What did your team's first agent eval suite get wrong?", "What do junior engineers on your team struggle with in their first six months?", "If you were in my position, which of my projects would you lead with?"
- **Take notes, and listen more than you talk.** Have a 30-second version of your story ready in case they ask, but the call is for learning.
- **Close well.** In the last couple of minutes, ask: "Is there anyone else you think I should talk to?" Introductions from a warm conversation are worth much more than cold messages.
- **Thank them within a day**, in a short message that mentions one specific thing you'll do with their advice.

Don't ask for a referral in an informational conversation with someone you just met. If they offer one, accept gratefully. Otherwise, the conversation is the point.

### Following up without being pushy

Silence after one message usually means "busy," not "no." A single follow-up is polite and often works. More than that starts to feel like pressure.

A good rule of thumb: if there's no reply after about a week, send one short follow-up in the same thread, so they don't have to search for context. Add something small and new if you can, such as a result or a link, rather than just "bumping this." Then stop. If there's still no reply, let it go without any resentment, and don't take it personally. You'll meet people at very different moments in their lives.

There's a second kind of follow-up that people forget, and it's the most valuable: **closing the loop**. A few weeks after someone gives you advice, tell them what you did with it. "You suggested I lead my portfolio with the cost project. I did, and two recruiters asked about it." People who give advice rarely hear what happened next. Closing the loop makes them invested in you, and it's how a single conversation turns into a relationship.

To do this reliably, you need to write things down. Keep a simple tracker of who you contacted, when, about what, and when to follow up. You'll build one in Running a Job Search and use it in Project: Outreach Sprint.

### How referrals work

Referrals come in different strengths, and it helps to know which one you're asking for.

- A **strong referral** comes from someone who has worked with you or seen your work closely, and can say why you'd be good: "I reviewed three of her PRs; she writes careful tests and takes feedback well." This carries real weight.
- A **weak referral** comes from someone who has just met you. Many employees will submit one anyway, often marked "I don't know this person well." It may still get your application read, but it says less.

Both are worth having. But a weak referral from a stranger, requested in a first message, is a poor trade: it costs them a favor and gives you little. Earn referrals instead of collecting them.

**When to ask.** Ask for a referral when three things are true: you've had a real interaction (a conversation, some collaboration, sustained help in a community), there's a specific open role you match well, and you've done your homework on that role. "Would you refer me to anything at your company?" is hard to say yes to. "Would you be comfortable referring me for the AI engineer role posted last week?" is easy.

**Make it easy.** Send the link to the posting, your resume (Resume for AI Roles, the next lesson, shows how to tailor it), and three or four sentences the referrer can paste into the referral form: the role, your strongest piece of evidence for it, and how you know each other. Most referral forms ask "How do you know this person?", so give them an honest answer to use.

**Accept no gracefully.** Some people can't refer, have a policy against referring people they haven't worked with, or just don't feel they know you well enough. Thank them, and keep the relationship. Asking again in six months, after more interaction, is fine.

**Respect the referrer.** Your referral reflects on them. Prepare seriously, tell them when you hear back, and thank them whatever the outcome.
