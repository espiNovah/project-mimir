### Introduction

A job posting is a wish list written by a busy person, often copied from an older posting. Read literally, most postings ask for more than any one person has. Read carefully, they tell you exactly what a team needs, which skills are table stakes, and which ones would make you stand out. This lesson teaches you to read postings like data, and to keep your skills current in a field that changes every month without drowning in hype.

### Lesson overview

This section contains a general overview of topics that you will learn in this lesson.

- The anatomy of a job posting.
- Separating must-haves from nice-to-haves.
- Reading between the lines for stage, team and real responsibilities.
- Tallying skills across many postings to find what's in demand.
- Applying when you don't meet every requirement.
- Staying current with a small, reliable set of sources.

### Key ideas

- **Postings follow a pattern:** a pitch for the company, the responsibilities, the requirements, the "nice to haves," and logistics. The responsibilities section tells you the real job; the requirements are often inflated.
- **Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves.** Skills repeated in the title, the first responsibilities and the requirements are must-haves. A long tail of named tools is usually nice-to-have. If you have most of the must-haves, apply.
- **Look for clues about the team.** "Zero to one," "first AI hire" or "wear many hats" means an early team and broad scope. "Scale," "reliability" and "platform" mean a mature product. Mentions of customers and travel signal forward-deployed work.
- **One posting is an anecdote; twenty are data.** Collect postings for your target role and tally how often each skill appears. Python, evals, RAG, agents and tool use, cloud deployment and clear communication usually top the list. That tally tells you where to focus your portfolio.
- **Tools change, concepts last.** A posting that names one framework will usually hire someone who knows a comparable one well and can show they learn fast. Translate tool names into concepts ("LangGraph" is agent orchestration) and point to where you've done that concept.
- **Stay current with a few good sources, on a schedule.** A daily newsletter digest and one or two trusted practitioners are enough. Skim weekly, go deep only on what affects your work, and try new things by building small demos rather than reading takes.

   - Skim one week of issues and write down the one development that most affects the work you'd do in your target role.
1. Bookmark [Simon Willison's blog](https://simonwillison.net/), one of the most reliable practitioners writing about LLMs, with frequent hands-on notes on new models and tools.
   - Read his five most recent posts and note how he tests new tools by building small things.
1. Find five real postings for your target role. For each, highlight the must-haves, list the nice-to-haves, and write one sentence on what the team actually needs. Keep these; they'll be the first five of the twenty in Project: Role Map.
